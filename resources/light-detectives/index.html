Notice: a hot glowing thing makes a continuous rainbow with no gaps. Hotter objects pump out more blue light. That's why the hottest stars look blue-white and cooler stars look orange-red.
For the curious: how the math works

The color where the glow is brightest moves with temperature (Wien's law):

peak wavelength = 2,898,000 nm·K ÷ temperature

And hotter objects give off far more light per square meter: double the temperature means 2⁴ = 16× more light (Stefan–Boltzmann law).

light per m² ∝ temperature⁴

Where colors come from

Glowing gas is different. It only gives off a few exact colors. Why?

ModelElectrons in an atom can only sit on certain "steps" (energy levels), like a ladder. When an electron jumps down, the atom shoots out a bit of light (a photon). Bigger jump = more energy = bluer light.

You built hydrogen's fingerprint!

These 4 lines are the same ones you see in the hydrogen tube. Every hydrogen atom in the universe makes exactly these colors. Other elements have different ladders, so they make different patterns.
For the curious: how the math works

Hydrogen's steps have energies E = −13.6 eV ÷ n². The light's energy is the gap between the steps, and its wavelength is:

wavelength (nm) = 1240 ÷ energy gap (eV)

Measure it like a scientist

Turn your phone into a spectrometer with phyphox.

Your eye sees the colors. A phone camera behind the grating can measure them. phyphox's Spectroscopy experiment averages the picture into a graph: brightness vs. position on the camera chip (in pixels). But pixels aren't colors yet. You have to calibrate: teach the graph which pixel is which wavelength, using lines you already know.

The trick: use the hydrogen lines you already built in Mission 2. Hydrogen's aqua Hβ line is at 486 nm and its bright red Hα line is at 656 nm. Find them, tell the graph, and every other pixel falls into place.

Real phyphox tips (for your phone)
  • Open phyphox → find the Spectroscopy experiment (camera section). Allow camera access.
  • Rubber-band the phone so the main camera sits flat against the grating, looking down the tube at the slit.
  • Rotate until the rainbow runs across the screen, then lock it in place. If the phone moves, recalibrate.
  • Point at the hydrogen tube, tap its red and aqua peaks, and enter 656 and 486. (The violet lines are faint and may not show up on a phone. That's fine; you only need two.)
  • Backup: the mercury tube's blue 436 nm and green 546 nm lines work too. Old-style fluorescent lights contain mercury; LED lights don't.
  • Now point at other tubes and write down the wavelengths of the peaks. Bring them to Mission 4!
  • No phone? No problem. Use your eyes and your worksheet, or pair up.
For the curious: how a grating works

A grating has about 1,000 tiny grooves per millimeter, so the grooves are 1,000 nm apart. Light bends by an angle that depends on its wavelength:

sin(angle) = wavelength ÷ groove spacing

Red bends more than violet, and that spreads the colors apart.

5

Reading starlight

Now point it at the sky. These are real spectra of stars.

A. Why stars have dark lines

A star is a hot glowing ball (rainbow) wrapped in a thinner, cooler gas layer. That gas absorbs its own fingerprint colors, leaving dark lines in exactly the spots where it would make bright ones.

B. What is the Sun made of?

Real dataSpectrum of a star just like the Sun (type G2). Turn on each element's fingerprint. If its lines land on dark dips, that element is in the star!

    The element found in the Sun first

    In 1868, astronomers watching a solar eclipse saw a bright yellow line at 587.6 nm that matched no element known on Earth. They named the mystery element after the Greek word for the Sun, helios. Helium wasn't found on Earth until 1895. Try the helium fingerprint above: it doesn't show up as dips in ordinary sunlight, which is why it hid for so long.

    C. Sort the stars by temperature

    Real dataSeven real star spectra, each scaled so its brightest point reaches the top. Hotter stars give off more blue; cooler stars give off more red. Tap the stars in order from hottest to coolest.

    D. A star that breaks the rules

    Real data · SDSSThis is SDSS J215018.83+003547.8, a dwarf carbon star. Normal stars have more oxygen than carbon. This one has more carbon, which forms molecules like C₂ that carve wide, sawtooth bands into the spectrum. Dwarf carbon stars probably got their carbon from a companion star that has since died, which makes them clues to how pairs of stars live and die together.

    E. Is that star moving?

    Simulated shift of real dataWhen a star moves away from us, all its lines slide toward red. Moving toward us, they slide toward blue (the Doppler effect, like a passing siren). We took a real hot star and gave it a secret speed. Slide the lab fingerprint until the hydrogen lines line up with the dips.

    For the curious: how the math works
    speed = speed of light × (shift ÷ lab wavelength)

    The speed of light is 300,000 km/s.
