Light Detectives

We can never visit a star, but we can catch its light. Every element leaves its own barcode of colors in that light. In this lab you'll photograph glowing gases through your own spectroscope, turn the photos into real measurements, identify a mystery gas, and then read the barcodes hidden in starlight.

Rainbows and lines

A diffraction grating spreads light out by color. What you see depends on what made the light.
Predict first: you look at a glowing tube of hydrogen gas through your grating. What will you see?

Three kinds of spectra: a hot, glowing solid (like a bulb filament) makes a continuous rainbow. A thin glowing gas makes bright lines. A cool gas in front of a bright light makes dark lines at exactly the same colors. You'll use the bright lines today, and the dark lines when we get to stars.

Where the lines come from

Why does a gas give off only a few exact colors?

ModelElectrons in an atom can only sit on certain "steps" (energy levels), like a ladder. When an electron jumps down, the atom shoots out a bit of light (a photon). Bigger jump = more energy = bluer light.

These are the Balmer lines: Hα (red, 3→2), Hβ (aqua, 4→2), Hγ (5→2) and Hδ (6→2, both violet). They're the same lines you'll photograph in the hydrogen tube, and you'll use them to calibrate your spectroscope in Mission 4. Other elements have different ladders, so they make different patterns.
For the curious: how the math works

Hydrogen's steps have energies E = −13.6 eV ÷ n². The light's energy is the gap between the steps, and its wavelength is:

wavelength (nm) = 1240 ÷ energy gap (eV)

From photo to spectrum

Turn a picture of colored lines into a real measurement.

Take a photo of a glowing tube through your spectroscope, then load it here. The page lines up the rainbow and adds up the brightness at each spot along it. That turns the 2D photo into a 1D spectrum: brightness vs. position on the camera.

What's in your photo? The bright stripe in the middle, inside a glow, is the slit itself: light that went straight through the grating (astronomers call it the "zero order"). The grating bends each color off to the side, so you get a copy of the spectrum on each side, mirror images, with violet closest to the slit and red farthest out. You may also see a faint smear of extra colors and dim "ghost" lines from light bouncing around inside the tube. That's normal; the page knows to look for the sharp lines.

🔒 Your photos never leave your device. All the measuring happens right here in your browser.

📱 Taking a good spectrum photo
  • Lock the phone to the spectroscope (rubber bands or tape) so the camera looks through the grating at the slit. Don't move it between photos. That's what lets one calibration work for every photo.
  • Use the normal camera at 1×. Don't zoom. On iPhone Pro models, turn Macro off so the phone doesn't switch lenses.
  • Darken the room. Tap the screen on the colored lines to focus, then slide the brightness down a little so the bright lines aren't washed out to white.
  • Turn the phone until the colors spread sideways across the screen, with the bright slit in the middle and a rainbow copy on each side.
  • Sharper lines: make the slit narrow (two pieces of tape or card edges almost touching), put the slit right up against the glowing tube, and line the inside of the tube with black paper to cut down on ghosts.
  • Take one photo per tube: hydrogen first, then the others.

Your 1D spectrum

Check yourself: why do the lines look straight up-and-down in the photo, but become peaks in the graph?
How the page builds a 1D spectrum
  1. Straighten. The page rotates the photo so the colors run exactly left to right. Auto-straighten tries many small tilts and keeps the one where the lines come out sharpest.
  2. Pick the band. Only the rows inside the amber box are used. That keeps out the dark sky around the lines and any glare.
  3. Add down each column. For every column of pixels, the page adds up the brightness of each pixel in the band. A colored line is bright in every row, so its column adds up to a tall peak.
  4. Find the peaks. A peak counts if it's the brightest spot nearby and stands well above its surroundings. Its exact center is the balance point of the bright pixels, so even a washed-out line gets a precise position.

For the curious: how the calibration works
  1. The grating equation. A grating with grooves d apart bends light of wavelength λ by an angle θ where
    sin θ = λ ÷ d   (d = 1,000,000 nm ÷ lines per mm)
  2. The camera turns angles into pixels. A line bent by θ lands a distance from the center of the pattern of
    distance = F × tan θ   where F depends on your phone's lens.
  3. Two known lines pin it down. Each Balmer line you identify gives one (pixel, wavelength) pair. With two, the page solves for F and the exact center. Trying both common gratings, it keeps the one whose center lands on the slit you photographed.
  4. Every later photo uses the same F, measured from wherever the slit lands in that photo. So small shifts of the phone are handled automatically, but tilting or zooming the camera is not.
  5. If the page can't find the slit (for example, it's cropped out), it falls back to a simple straight-line scale between your two lines.

6

Barcodes in starlight

Now point it at the sky. These are real spectra of stars.

A star is a hot glowing ball wrapped in thinner gas. That gas absorbs its own fingerprint colors, so star spectra show dark lines in exactly the places your gas tubes made bright ones. Same barcodes, opposite color.

A. What is the Sun made of?

Real dataSpectrum of a star just like the Sun (type G2). Turn on each element's fingerprint. If its lines land on dark dips, that element is in the star!

    The element found in the Sun first

    In 1868, astronomers watching a solar eclipse saw a bright yellow line at 587.6 nm that matched no element known on Earth. They named the mystery element after the Greek word for the Sun, helios. Helium wasn't found on Earth until 1895. Check your helium tube's brightest line: it's that same yellow line. Then try the helium fingerprint above: it doesn't show up as dips in ordinary sunlight, which is why it hid for so long.

    B. Different stars, different barcodes

    Real dataSeven real stars, from the hottest type (O) to the coolest (M). Turn on fingerprints and hunt for the lines.

    Why do the barcodes change? Every star contains hydrogen, but a line only shows up if the gas is at the right conditions to absorb it. Hydrogen's visible lines are strongest around 10,000 K (type A). In the hottest stars the hydrogen is too stirred up to absorb these colors; in the coolest stars it's too calm. Cool stars are cool enough for atoms to stick together into molecules like titanium oxide (TiO), which carve wide bands. So a star's barcode tells you what it's made of and what conditions its gas is in.

    C. A star that breaks the rules

    Real data · SDSSThis is SDSS J215018.83+003547.8, a dwarf carbon star. Normal stars have more oxygen than carbon. This one has more carbon, which forms molecules like C₂ that carve wide, sawtooth bands into the spectrum. Dwarf carbon stars probably got their carbon from a companion star that has since died, which makes them clues to how pairs of stars live and die together.
