Part B: Transit Detective

Open Transit Detective from the link your instructor shares. With your partner, work through all six stars in order, A to F. They get harder as you go, so start with Star A as a warm-up. For each star, mark the dips, fold to find the period, and measure the depth. Fill in that star’s row of the table before you press Reveal.

Check the tool's math for Star A. The fraction of light blocked equals the planet's disk area divided by the star's disk area:

depth = (Rplanet / Rstar)2  ⇒  Rplanet = Rstar × √depth × 109

The 109 converts Sun radii into Earth radii. Write the depth as a decimal (1% = 0.01).

Planet radius = ______ × √______ × 109 = ______ Earth radii

Questions

1. How did you decide your period was correct? What did the folded plot look like when it was wrong?

2. Star C's dips are hard to see in the raw data but obvious after folding. Why does stacking many transits on top of each other help?

3. Star C's planet is about the size of Earth, yet TESS found it easily. What about Star C made that possible?

4. What clues told you that Star F is not a planet? List at least two.

Part C: Putting it together

LHS 3844 b is about the size of Earth, and its dip is 0.4% deep. An identical planet passing in front of the Sun would make a dip of only about 0.008%. Why is the dip so much smaller?

Part D: Real planet hunting (on your own)

If you run out of time in class, do this part on your own. Go to Planet Hunters TESS on Zooniverse. Volunteers look for transits that computer searches can miss, and the science team follows up the best candidates. Do the short tutorial, then classify at least five.
