Light Buckets

You have two lenses: a big one and a small one. By the end of this lab you'll know how they bend light, how to measure them, and how to put them together into a working telescope that you take home.

Before you start

Your kit (work with a partner):

  • 2 lenses: a big one (5 cm wide) and a small one (3 cm wide)
  • a meter stick (or a 30 cm ruler plus a tape measure)
  • a white index card
  • your worksheet and a pencil
  • a phone or laptop with this page open

Set up:

  1. Put a small piece of tape on the edge of the big lens and write A. The small lens is B. Your partner does the same with their lenses.
  2. Hold lenses by their edges. Fingerprints on the glass make everything blurry.
  3. Find a bright window or a lamp on the far side of the room. You'll need it in Steps 5, 6 and 8.
Safety: never look at the Sun through a lens or a telescope, and never use a lens to focus sunlight on anything (it can start a fire or hurt your eyes).
Your plan for today: Steps 1–8 are the core (about 35 minutes; the ⏱ tags show roughly how long each takes). Steps 9 and 10 are bonus: do them if you finish early, or at home. Then you build your own telescope (Step 11) with the paper build guide.

Light travels in straight lines

Before we can bend light, we need a way to draw it. ⏱ 2 min

🎯 You'll learn: what a ray is, and why light from far away arrives "parallel".

Light leaves a glowing object, like a light bulb, in every direction. Each bit of light travels in a straight line until something changes its path. Scientists draw those paths as lines with arrows, called rays.

Light bends when it enters glass

This bending is called refraction. ⏱ 4 min

🎯 You'll learn: why light bends at a surface, and which way it bends.

Light travels slower in glass or water than in air. When a beam hits glass at a slant, one edge of the beam slows down first, so the whole beam turns. It's like a toy car rolling at an angle from pavement onto grass: the wheel that hits the grass first slows down and the car swerves.

For the curious: the bending rule

Each material has an index of refraction, n: how many times slower light travels in it (air ≈ 1, water 1.33, glass 1.5, diamond 2.42). The angles follow Snell's law:

n(air) × sin(angle in air) = n(glass) × sin(angle in glass)
3

The shape decides where light goes

Same glass, different shapes, very different results. ⏱ 3 min

🎯 You'll learn: how the shape of a piece of glass decides where light goes, and what a converging lens is.

Parallel rays come in from the left, like starlight. Each ray bends where it enters and leaves the glass, by an amount that depends on how tilted the glass surface is at that spot. Pick a shape and watch.

In a lens that is fat in the middle, which rays bend the most?
Key ideaA lens that is fatter in the middle is a converging lens: its curved faces bend the outer rays most, so parallel rays all meet at one point. Telescopes use converging lenses.
🖐 With your lenses · Worksheet Step 3
  1. Hold each lens edge-on at eye level in front of a sheet of white paper. Look at its outline: is the middle thicker than the edge?
  2. Gently feel the faces between your thumb and finger (or just look). Which lens bulges more?
  3. Measure the width of each lens across the glass, in millimeters (mm), with your ruler. Don't include any plastic holder.
  4. Fill in the table on your worksheet.
4

The focal point

Where a converging lens brings parallel light together. ⏱ 2 min

🎯 You'll learn: what focal length means, and what decides it.

A converging lens bends every parallel ray toward the same spot: the focal point (F). The distance from the middle of the lens to that spot is the lens's most important number, its focal length, written f. Two things set it: how curved the faces are, and what the lens is made of.

For a lens with two faces curved the same amount (each face part of a ball with radius R):

f = R ÷ (2 × (n − 1))
5

Lenses make pictures

Called images. They can land on a piece of paper. ⏱ 4 min

🎯 You'll learn: how a lens makes a picture, why it's upside down, and where the sharp spot is.

Every point on a candle flame sends rays in all directions. The lens catches some of them and bends them back together at one point on the other side. Do that for every point on the candle and you get a picture: an image. Follow the bright yellow ray: it leaves the top of the flame, goes straight through the middle of the lens, and lands at the bottom. That's why the picture is upside down.

This lens has f = 10 cm. Drag the candle and the paper, or use the sliders. Heights are stretched so you can see the rays.

🖐 With your lenses · Worksheet Step 5
  1. Stand 3 m or more from a bright window (or a lamp). Turn your back to it if the room is bright.
  2. Hold Lens A up, facing the window. Hold the white card right behind the lens, touching it.
  3. Slowly pull the card away from the lens. At about a ruler's length (30 cm), a small, sharp picture of the window appears. Go past it and it blurs again.
  4. Is the picture upside down? Sketch it on your worksheet.
  5. Bonus: try a lamp only about 1 m away. Is the sharp spot closer to the lens or farther away than for the window?
For the curious: the lens equation

The object distance (do), picture distance (di) and focal length (f) are linked:

1 ÷ f = 1 ÷ do + 1 ÷ di

Measure a focal length

The trick: use something far away. ⏱ 6 min

🎯 You'll learn: how to measure f for a lens you know nothing about, and then measure both of yours.

Put Steps 1 and 4 together. Light from far away arrives as parallel rays, and a lens brings parallel rays together at its focal point. So when you focus a faraway window onto a card, the card is at the focal point, and the distance from the lens to the card is the focal length.

7

One lens: a magnifying glass

Close up, a converging lens makes things look bigger. ⏱ 4 min

🎯 You'll learn: how a magnifying glass works, and which of your lenses magnifies more.

When the object is closer to the lens than its focal length, the rays can't come together into a picture. They're still spreading after the lens, and your eye traces them back to a bigger, right-side-up picture behind the object (green). This is how a magnifying glass works, and it's exactly what the eyepiece of a telescope does.

Your eye is right behind the lens. Drag the bug (or use the slider) until it looks sharp. A healthy eye can't focus on anything closer than about 25 cm.

For the curious: magnifying power

Your eye sees clearly down to about 25 cm. A magnifying glass lets you bring things much closer and still see them sharp. Its best magnifying power (with the picture 25 cm away) is about

magnification ≈ 1 + 25 cm ÷ f
8

Two lenses: a telescope

The front lens makes a picture. The back lens magnifies it. ⏱ 8 min

🎯 You'll learn: how two lenses work together, how to plan a telescope, and how to build it.

  1. The objective (front lens) points at something far away. Just like in Step 6, it makes a small, upside-down image one focal length (fo) behind it.
  2. The eyepiece (back lens) is a magnifying glass, like in Step 7, looking at that image. It works best when the image is one eyepiece focal length (fe) in front of it.
  3. So the lenses go fo + fe apart, and the view is magnified fo ÷ fe times.

1609: Galileo's telescope

Galileo heard about Dutch "spyglasses" and built his own, eventually reaching about 20×. Pointing it at the sky, he saw mountains on the Moon, countless stars in the Milky Way, and four moons circling Jupiter. Your telescope is the same idea.
9

Light buckets

The real reason astronomers build giant telescopes. Bonus · 3 min

🎯 You'll learn: why a wider telescope shows fainter stars, and how much more light yours collects than your eye.

Most stars are too faint to see because only a little of their light lands in your eye. Your pupil opens to only about 7 mm in the dark. A telescope's objective is a much bigger opening, a light bucket, so it catches far more light from every star.

For the curious: why the square?

A lens collects light over its whole circle, and a circle's area grows with the square of its width: twice as wide → 2 × 2 = 4 times the light.

10

Why giant telescopes use mirrors

A curved mirror can focus light too. Bonus · 3 min

🎯 You'll learn: the problem with big lenses, and how mirrors fix it.

White light is a mix of colors. Glass bends blue light a little more than red light, so a lens brings the colors to slightly different focal points. A curved mirror bounces every color the same way.

Lens problems
  • Colors focus at different spots, so bright things get colored fringes.
  • Light passes through the glass, so all of it must be flawless.
  • A lens can only be held at its edges, so a huge one sags.
Mirror fixes
  • All colors bounce the same way: no fringes.
  • Light only touches the front surface.
  • A mirror can be supported from behind, or built from segments.

Drawn to scale: the width of each telescope's main lens or mirror, with a person's height (1.7 m) for comparison.

The biggest lens telescope ever used for astronomy (Yerkes, 1897) is about 1 m wide. The biggest telescope being built today is 39 m wide. What is it made of?
Key ideaLenses split colors and sag when they're big. The giant telescopes of today are mirror telescopes.
🖐 Look closely · Worksheet Step 10
Look through your telescope at a bright window frame or a white edge against something dark. Look along the edges: do you see faint red or blue fringes? That's the lens problem giant telescopes avoid by using mirrors.

My telescope report

The big ideas

  1. Light travels in straight lines; light from far away arrives parallel.
  2. Light bends when it enters or leaves glass.
  3. A lens fatter in the middle brings parallel light together at its focal point.
  4. Focal length f = lens to focal point. Measure it by focusing something far away.
  5. A lens makes an upside-down picture; closer than F, it magnifies instead.
  6. Telescope = long-f objective + short-f eyepiece, fo + fe apart, magnifying fo ÷ fe.
  7. Wider opening = more light = fainter stars. That's why telescopes are light buckets.
  8. To focus on closer things, move the eyepiece farther from the objective.
