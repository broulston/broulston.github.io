How Dwarf Carbon Stars Form

Ordinary-looking dwarf stars with atmospheres full of carbon they could never have made themselves. They are the fossil record of a companion star that lived, died, and left its ashes behind.

Background

The puzzle

Almost every star has more oxygen than carbon in its atmosphere. When a cool star has C/O > 1, the chemistry flips: the leftover carbon forms molecules like C2, CN and CH that dominate the spectrum. Giant stars can do this to themselves. Late in life, an asymptotic giant branch (AGB) star dredges freshly fused carbon up from its interior to its surface.

A dwarf carbon (dC) star shows the same carbon-rich spectrum, but it is a small, main-sequence K or M dwarf. It has never fused helium, so it cannot have made that carbon. The first one, G 77-61, was found in 1977. Within a decade it was shown to be a binary, pointing to the now-standard explanation: the carbon was donated by a companion that went through the AGB phase and is now a faint white dwarf.

That makes dC stars useful well beyond their own curiosity. They are thought to be the most numerous carbon stars in the Galaxy, and each one records a past episode of binary mass transfer. They may also be the progenitors of the better-known CH, Ba and CEMP-s stars. My research uses them to test how binary stars exchange mass.

How much carbon does it take?

Turning a dwarf into a dC is a dilution problem. Whatever carbon-rich gas the dwarf accretes gets mixed into its own envelope, which starts with C/O well below 1. The question at the heart of my current modeling work is how much material has to be accreted for the surface to end up with C/O > 1, and how that depends on the star's mass, its metallicity, and how carbon-rich the donor was.

Observations

What the observations tell us

Nearly all of them are binaries

G 77-61 was found to orbit an unseen companion every 245.5 days. With repeated SDSS spectra of 240 dC stars, we measured how often their radial velocities change. The data are consistent with essentially every dC being in a binary (a binary fraction of ~95%), with typical separations under 1 AU, or periods of order a year.

Dearborn et al. 1986; Roulston et al. 2019

The white dwarf is usually hidden

A white dwarf is Earth-sized and fades for billions of years, so in most systems it is invisible next to the dwarf. In about a dozen known dCs the white dwarf is still hot enough to show up in the optical spectrum, as a composite dC + white dwarf. Those systems caught the aftermath of mass transfer relatively recently.

Roulston et al. 2022 and references therein

Some are surprisingly close

In Zwicky Transient Facility light curves of 944 dCs, 34 vary periodically, and 82% of those have periods under 2 days. The shortest known dC period is just 3.2 hours. Orbits that tight almost certainly went through a common-envelope phase, with the dwarf's spin tidally locked to its orbit.

Roulston et al. 2021; Roulston et al. 2022

Spun up and magnetically active

Old dwarfs should be slow, quiet rotators, yet some dCs are X-ray sources. Among the five nearest dCs observed with Chandra, two were detected in X-rays, and those were the only two that are also short-period variables. Their activity is best explained by rapid rotation forced by a tight orbit.

Green et al. 2019; Roulston et al. 2022

An old, metal-poor population

In our Gaia DR3 census, dC stars have a vertical scale height of about 860 pc, much larger than for young disk stars. This fits an older population, consistent with the evidence that dCs tend to be metal-poor. That is just what the carbon budget predicts: with less oxygen to overcome, a little donated carbon goes a lot further at low metallicity.

Farihi et al. 2018; Roulston et al. 2025

Current research

Open questions I'm working on

How much mass transfer is actually needed?

I am running a large grid of MESA stellar evolution models, evolving each accreting star to the age of the Universe. The grid spans stellar mass, metallicity, how much material is accreted, and how carbon-rich that material is. Following the mixing over billions of years tells us which combinations still look like a dC star today, and how many of the binaries that should exist actually become one.

The common-envelope problem

The short-period dCs went through a common envelope, but models suggest the dwarf cannot accrete enough carbon during that brief phase. So the carbon likely arrived earlier, probably through wind Roche-lobe overflow, with the common envelope coming near the end of the donor's thermally pulsing AGB phase. How those two steps fit together is still open.

From a census to a population

Gaia's low-resolution spectra let us find dC stars across the whole sky, not just where earlier surveys happened to look. A well-defined sample, with a measured space density and scale height, is what population synthesis models of binary evolution have to reproduce. That comparison is how dC stars can test our picture of mass transfer.
